01 / LIVE DASHBOARD
AI · CYBERSECURITY · IOTPROJECT 01

SafeLab Sentinel

SafeLab Sentinel is an AI-powered laboratory monitoring and cybersecurity system designed to improve lab safety through real-time sensor monitoring, anomaly detection, and device authentication.

Built with Streamlit, FastAPI, and a Random Forest machine-learning model, it analyses temperature, air quality index (AQI), and vibration to identify potential hazards. HMAC-based message integrity checks, risk assessment, and SQLite event logging help flag suspicious device communications and preserve an event history. Simulated normal, hazardous, and cyberattack modes demonstrate how AI, cybersecurity, and industrial safety can work together.

02 / RESEARCH WORKFLOW
MACHINE LEARNING · MATERIALS SCIENCEPROJECT 02

Explainable ML for Carbon-Fibre Composites

This research project applies machine learning to predict and optimise the mechanical properties of carbon-fibre/Polyethersulfone (PSU) composite yarns. Using a dataset of 663 tensile-test specimens, it investigates how fibre concentration, specimen dimensions, mass, die size, processing load, and pre-tension influence tensile strength and related properties.

Multiple models, including Random Forest regression, are being explored to improve prediction accuracy. Explainable AI techniques help identify influential factors, while planned work includes failure-pattern classification and multi-objective optimisation to maximise strength and stiffness while minimising material mass. The project combines materials science, data analytics, and AI to support more informed composite design. I am working under a professor at VIT Vellore, with the aim of contributing to a research paper for publication.

STUDENT REFLECTION · INDEPENDENT PORTFOLIO WRITING

Beyond the Screen: Why Cybersecurity Also Depends on the Physical World

Author: Yaakshitaa SathiyamurthyPerspective: First-year undergraduateTopic: Cybersecurity & connected devices

Context & learning: This reflection brings together ideas I am beginning to learn through cybersecurity, engineering physics, and my SafeLab Sentinel project. It is not a formal research paper; it is my current understanding of why protecting digital systems involves more than just writing secure code.

REFLECTION

Security does not stop at software

When I first started learning about cybersecurity, I mostly thought about passwords, malicious links, and code. Those things are important, but I am beginning to understand that a digital system is also connected to the physical world around it. A computer depends on hardware, a network depends on equipment, and an IoT device may depend on sensors that measure temperature, movement, or air quality. If one part of that chain is unreliable or compromised, the information we see on a screen may not tell the whole story.

This became easier for me to picture while working on SafeLab Sentinel, a prototype that brings together simulated sensor readings, anomaly detection, message-integrity checks, and risk scoring. A sensor value by itself is only a number. To make a useful decision, a system also needs to consider whether the reading looks unusual, whether the message may have been changed, and what the reading could mean for the environment being monitored. I am still learning how to design these checks properly, but the project has helped me see why safety and cybersecurity can overlap.

Small signals can matter

Engineering Physics has also made me think about the hardware underneath software. Electrical signals, electromagnetic effects, and the behaviour of physical components can influence how devices operate. I am not yet an expert in hardware security, but I find it interesting that a system can reveal information through more than its normal digital output. Topics such as side-channel attacks show that timing, power use, or electromagnetic behaviour may sometimes provide clues that were not meant to be exposed.

For me, the lesson is not that every device is unsafe. It is that security needs to be considered at different layers. A strong password cannot fix a faulty sensor, and a correct machine-learning prediction cannot automatically prove that the data supplied to it is trustworthy. Good design involves asking where information comes from, how it travels, what checks protect it, and how a system should respond when something seems wrong.

What I am taking forward

As a first-year student, I am still building the foundations in programming, physics, and security that will help me understand these problems in greater depth. For now, I want to keep asking practical questions while I build: What could go wrong? Which part of the system do I trust, and why? How would I notice an unusual event? Those questions make technical learning feel connected to real life, and they give me a direction for the projects I want to explore next.

My takeaway

Cybersecurity is not only about protecting code. It is also about understanding the devices, data, and physical conditions that code depends on.
